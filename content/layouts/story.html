"I grew up where the land dissolves into the water."

The Alabama Gulf Coast — cypress swamps, brackish bayous, the kind of biodiversity that gets into your bones. That environment taught me early what healthy water looks like, and what it means when it isn't.

I moved north and found Baltimore, a city with a complicated relationship with its waterways. Through Blue Water Baltimore and AmeriCorps, I spent two years focused on giving communities access to the same ecological richness I'd grown up with. It wasn't just a job. It was the beginning of a mission.

At Maryland Environmental Service, I taught myself GIS from scratch — no formal training, just problems that needed solving and tools that could solve them. I built workflows, ran infrastructure resilience inspections, and worked on stormwater BMP programs across the state. The technical side of environmental work started to feel as natural as the fieldwork.

In 2017, Hurricane Maria made landfall in Puerto Rico. I joined CrowdRescue HQ as a volunteer, mapping resource needs in real time. What started as volunteer GIS work escalated fast — I ended up running the geospatial infrastructure, sitting in on coordination calls at FEMA HQ, attending a White House workshop on information flows during natural disasters, and contributing to Geospatial Consortium standards for emergency response data. It remains one of the most consequential things I've ever done.

COVID collapsed the environmental field. I lost my position and spent time finding my footing — but I never left the mission. I came back through a different door: wastewater treatment plant operations. I started as an Operator-in-Training and earned my Maryland Wastewater 5A license — the highest grade in the state — in under two years. Now, as an Operator III with Howard County Government, I protect the Chesapeake Bay watershed every day. And I'm training the next generation of operators through our apprenticeship program.

The tools change. The water doesn't.
