"I grew up where the land dissolves into the water."
[ Origin ]

The Alabama Gulf Coast — brackish bayous, cypress swamps, the barrier island of Dauphin Island standing guard against the Gulf. Sea oats bending in the wind at Gulf Shores. Piers and wharves extending into Mobile Bay, boats of every size threading between them.

And the jubilees. On summer nights along the Eastern Shore of Mobile Bay, word would spread faster than the tide: the fish are coming up. Flounder, shrimp, blue crabs — pushed to the shallows by low oxygen in the deeper water — would surge to shore. Families waded in with lanterns and baskets and took what the Bay gave. It was a communion. You don't waste something like that. You don't poison it.

Mardi Gras down there isn't just a party — it's a seasonal ritual that binds communities to each other and to the water. The whole culture of the Gulf Coast is built around that relationship. We took care of our waterways because our waterways were expected to sustain us. That wasn't taught in a classroom. It was lived.

[ Baltimore ]

I moved north and found Baltimore — a city with a complicated and often resigned relationship with its waterways. Through Blue Water Baltimore and AmeriCorps, I spent two years on the ground monitoring the streams of the Baltimore watershed: the Jones Falls, the Gwynns Falls, the Patapsco River in both its tidal and non-tidal reaches.

The work was unglamorous. I trekked through urban landscapes and industrial corridors, screening stormwater outfalls for sewage contamination — pulling samples, logging data, filing reports with the city. It was a thankless job in a lot of ways. But what struck me harder than the pollution itself was the attitude around it. People in Baltimore had simply accepted that their water was dirty. Some got angry when you even raised the subject. The idea that a stream running through their neighborhood could be swimmable, fishable — something to actually be proud of — felt foreign to them in a way it never would on the Gulf Coast.

Growing up on Mobile Bay, clean water wasn't an environmental cause. It was a birthright. Bringing that expectation north — the stubborn refusal to accept dirty water as normal — was the beginning of understanding that this work was always about more than ecology. It was about equity. About giving communities access to something they'd never thought to expect.

[ The GIS Years ]

At Maryland Environmental Service, I found a new set of tools. I taught myself GIS from scratch — no formal training, just problems that needed solving and software that could solve them. I built workflow tools for clients, led infrastructure resilience inspections, and ran stormwater BMP programs across the state. The technical side of environmental work started to feel as natural as the fieldwork.

Learning GIS that way — through necessity, through iteration, through breaking things and rebuilding them — turned out to shape how I think about every technical problem. If you understand what you're trying to say, you can usually find a way to say it.

[ Maria & FEMA ]

In October 2017, Hurricane Maria made landfall in Puerto Rico. I joined CrowdRescue HQ as a volunteer — armed with self-taught skills and the backing of a crowdsourced community pulling data from across the internet and stitching it into something coherent.

I felt like a fish out of water the entire time. But the work didn't care how I felt. What started as volunteer mapping escalated fast and without warning. The geospatial data we were producing — a visual tapestry assembled from satellite imagery, social media reports, field teams, and remote sensing — ended up distributed across the Federal Government. It landed on the desk of the Chief of the Army Corps of Engineers. Daily.

Suddenly I was expected to be a subject matter expert. Federal agencies wanted my input. Researchers wanted to study what we'd built. I attended coordination calls at FEMA HQ, participated in a White House workshop on information flows during natural disasters, and contributed to Geospatial Consortium standards for emergency response data. It remains one of the most consequential stretches of my life.

[ The Pivot ]

COVID collapsed the environmental sector. I lost my position and spent time finding my footing — stormwater inspections, logistics work, whatever kept me connected. But the mission never left.

When I decided to pursue wastewater operations, it felt inevitable. Like things had come full circle. I understood enough of the system — the science, the infrastructure, the regulatory framework — to be confident I could succeed. And I believed that being inside the plant, operating the systems that actually protect the watershed, was the most direct path to being the change I wanted to see.

Starting over from scratch was humbling. People would look at my background and ask, plainly, what I was doing there. My ability to absorb and apply the technical material made me stand out — in good ways and in ways that created friction. It was a rough few years. But the license was always the goal. When I crossed that line — Maryland Wastewater 5A, the highest operator grade in the state — everything clicked. The standard track requires three years of experience. My B.A. in Environmental Sustainability reduced that requirement to two, putting the license in hand a full year ahead of schedule. It was vindication. And it was a beginning.

[ Now ]

I do my rounds. I run the process sampling and laboratory work that keeps the plant running clean. Some shifts I'm running the facility, with operators on temporary licenses working under mine — a responsibility I take seriously. The Little Patuxent Water Reclamation Plant (LPWRP) discharges to the Patuxent River, a Chesapeake Bay tributary. Every sample I pull and every process decision I make connects to that watershed.

Mentoring the next generation means more than passing on procedures. It means giving people the tools to succeed and helping them calibrate for what this field will actually throw at them — in five years, in ten, in twenty. What changes. What holds. What they'll wish someone had told them early.

My core teaching: any skill is applicable to wastewater. You just have to build the connections and be creative enough to adapt what you know to what the plant needs. My career is proof of that.

The tools change. The water doesn't.
